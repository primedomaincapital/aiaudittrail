The Law That Never Started: Colorado’s AI Act Died, and Texas Took Its Place

Colorado’s AI Act was the reference point for American AI regulation for two years. It was repealed on May 14, 2026, before it ever applied to anyone. The laws that did take effect on January 1, 2026 look nothing like it — and the audit trail they demand is different too.

by prime domain capital · 2026-11-09

Somewhere in the first quarter of 2026, a compliance officer at a mid-size insurer finished a project plan she had been building for eighteen months. It mapped every high-risk AI system in the company, assigned impact assessments to each one, and set a June 30 deadline — the day Colorado’s AI Act was supposed to take effect. Six weeks later, the law was gone. Not delayed. Not amended. Repealed and replaced, before it ever applied to a single company.

That is the story of American AI regulation in 2026: the law everyone prepared for never started, and the laws nobody built their program around are now the ones that count. The audit trail a company needs today is not the one its lawyers described in 2024. It is shorter, sharper, and in some respects harder — because it has to survive in a system where the rules change faster than the systems they govern.

What happened in Colorado

Colorado passed SB 24-205 in May 2024, with Governor Jared Polis signing it while publicly asking the legislature to refine it before it took effect. For two years it was the reference point: the first comprehensive American law regulating high-risk AI, built around impact assessments and algorithmic discrimination. Its start date slipped from February 1 to June 30, 2026. Then, on April 27, 2026, a federal court approved a stay in xAI’s constitutional challenge to the law, with the U.S. Department of Justice intervening in support of the challenge. Twelve days later, on May 9, the legislature passed SB 26-189, a bipartisan rewrite. Polis signed it on May 14. SB 24-205 was expressly superseded, having never applied to anyone.

The replacement is a different kind of law. It drops the comprehensive high-risk framework and rebuilds around automated decision-making technology in consequential decisions — hiring, lending, housing, insurance, education. Developers and deployers doing business in Colorado owe notice to affected people, an explanation of an adverse outcome within 30 days, a chance to correct the record, human review, and three years of records. It takes effect January 1, 2027 and applies to decisions made on or after that date. Enforcement sits with the attorney general alone, with a 60-day cure period running until 2030. The rules are still being written: the Colorado Department of Law filed proposed rules on August 11, 2026, released an interim draft on October 6, and held its formal hearing on October 26, with final rules due by January 1, 2027.

What actually took effect on January 1, 2026

While Colorado was rewriting, three other regimes quietly switched on. Texas’s Responsible AI Governance Act, HB 149, signed June 22, 2025, took effect January 1, 2026. It is the broadest of the state laws in a specific sense: it applies to anyone doing business in Texas or serving Texas residents, with no size threshold at all. Rather than regulating high-risk systems by category, it prohibits AI used for enumerated purposes — encouraging self-harm, infringing rights, unlawful discrimination, child sexual abuse material, non-consensual deepfakes, government social scoring — and requires government agencies to disclose when residents interact with AI. Enforcement is by the attorney general alone, with a 60-day cure and penalties of $10,000 to $200,000 per violation.

Illinois’ HB 3773 took effect the same day, and California’s frontier-model law, SB 53, signed September 29, 2025, is in force as well, alongside the state’s existing employment rules on automated-decision systems and New York City’s Local Law 144 bias audits. The two workload-changing deadlines both land on January 1, 2027: California’s CCPA rules on automated decisionmaking technology, and Colorado’s rewritten law. As of late October 2026, the honest summary is that Texas and Illinois are in force now, the big documentation duties start in 2027, and every deadline is provisional — a federal executive order issued December 11, 2025 set the Department of Justice to challenging several of these laws in court.

The case against the patchwork

The counterargument writes itself, and it is not frivolous. A company operating in all fifty states now faces a Texas law that bans specific conduct, a Colorado law that demands notice and records, a California regime aimed at frontier models, and a federal government arguing in court that states should not be writing AI law at all. Compliance officers call it unworkable. Trade groups call it a tax on building in America. There is a real chance a court agrees with them, at least in part.

But “probably preempted eventually” is not a compliance posture. Until a court says otherwise, TRAIGA is enforceable against a company of any size that serves a single Texas resident, and Colorado’s 2027 duties are on the calendar with rulemaking already underway. The firms that treated SB 24-205 as the template and built impact-assessment machinery for it now have to remap that work onto a notice-and-records law — different artifacts, different owners, different retention. The ones that built nothing, waiting for federal clarity, have neither.

What the audit trail looks like now

The practical lesson is narrower than the politics. The records that matter under the laws in force are not the elaborate impact assessments Colorado originally demanded. They are the boring ones: what system made which decision, on what date, using which model version, with what notice given to the person affected, and where the record lives for three years. Texas adds a different discipline — proof that a system was not built or deployed for a prohibited purpose, which is a design record, not a monitoring dashboard. California’s 2027 rules will add a third set.

Build the trail to survive a change of statute. A log that records the decision, the model version, the notice, and the human review works under Colorado’s rewrite, under California’s coming rules, and under any federal framework that eventually replaces both. A compliance program built around a single law’s vocabulary dies with that law — as the teams that spent eighteen months preparing for June 30, 2026 just learned.
