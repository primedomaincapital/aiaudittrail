How Long Must Companies Keep AI Decision Records?

Europe says six months for logs and ten years for the paperwork. The United States says nothing at all. The gap between those two answers is where the audit trail lives — or dies.

by Prime Domain Capital Research · 2026-11-19

The regulator’s letter arrived on a Tuesday, asking a bank to explain a lending decision its model had made three years earlier. The bank had the model. It had the current version of the model. It did not have the version that had made the decision, the data the version had been trained on, or the log of the inputs that had produced the outcome. The retention policy, written before the bank deployed any AI at all, said seven years for records. Nobody had decided whether a model’s decision log was a record. The bank spent six months and a seven-figure legal bill reconstructing what it could have kept for the price of storage, and the regulator’s conclusion was not that the decision was wrong. It was that the bank could not prove it was right.

That is the question this article answers, and it is the question every company running AI in production will be asked: how long must you keep the records of what your models decided? The answer depends on where you operate, what your model does, and which regulator is asking. Europe has an answer, measured in months and years. The United States has no federal answer at all. The standards that sit between them, NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework and ISO/IEC 42001, tell you to keep records without telling you for how long. The gap between those answers is where the audit trail lives, or dies.

The European answer, in months and years

The European Union’s AI Act is the only law in the world that sets a retention period for AI decision records in so many words, and it sets two of them. Article 12 requires high-risk AI systems to automatically record their operation, logging the events that matter: when the system was used, what data it processed, and what it decided. Article 19 requires the provider to keep those logs for at least six months, and Article 26 requires the deployer to keep them for at least six months as well. Article 18 sets the longer period: the technical documentation for a high-risk system, the description of the model, its training data, its testing, and its risk management, must be kept for ten years after the system is placed on the market or put into service.

The dates matter as much as the periods. The AI Act’s obligations for general-purpose AI models took effect on August 2, 2025. The high-risk obligations for the systems listed in Annex III, which include employment, credit scoring, and insurance pricing, were scheduled for August 2, 2026, but the Digital Omnibus Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, moved them to December 2, 2027, and the Annex I obligations, for products already covered by existing EU law, to August 2, 2028. The delay is not a reprieve. It is a timetable, and the companies that use it to build their logging now will be ready when the clock starts, while the companies that treat it as a cancellation will be the ones explaining to a regulator why the logs from 2026 no longer exist.

The six-month floor is the number most companies get wrong, because it sounds short. Six months is the minimum, not the target. A lending decision challenged three years later, an employment decision challenged after a hiring cycle, a claim denied and litigated two years after the fact, none of these fit inside six months. The ten-year documentation period is the more honest guide to what the law expects: the model’s paperwork outlives the model’s logs, because the question a regulator asks years later is usually about the system, not the single decision.

The American answer: there isn’t one

The United States has no federal AI record-retention law. There is no statute that says how long a company must keep the logs of what its models decided, and no federal regulator has issued a retention rule for AI systems as such. What exists instead is the retention schedule the company already has, written for a world of paper records and email, applied by analogy to a technology it was never written for. The analogy usually produces the wrong answer.

The established schedules are the ones to start from. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act requires audit work papers to be kept for seven years. The Securities and Exchange Commission’s Rule 17a-4 requires broker-dealers to keep records for three to six years, depending on the record. The health insurance portability rules require six years. These are real numbers with real enforcement behind them, and they are the numbers a company’s general counsel will reach for first. But none of them was written for a model that makes ten thousand decisions a day, and none of them answers the question of whether a decision log is a business record, a model artifact, or something else entirely.

The state laws are filling the gap unevenly. Colorado’s AI Act, the first comprehensive state AI law, imposes documentation and impact-assessment duties on deployers of high-risk AI, and its effective date has been delayed repeatedly as the legislature argues over it. California, Illinois, and a dozen other states have their own bills in various stages. The result is a map, not a rule: a company operating in all fifty states faces fifty different answers to the same question, and the only safe retention period is the longest one on the map.

What the standards say — and what they don’t

The two standards that govern AI audit trails, the ones the last article in this series compared, are both silent on the number. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework, the voluntary guidance that the bank in the opening story had built its program on, tells organizations to govern, map, measure, and manage their AI risk, and to document what they do. It does not say for how long. ISO/IEC 42001, the certifiable management system standard, requires documented information and records of the system’s operation, and it requires the organization to define its own retention periods. The standard is a framework for deciding, not a decision.

That silence is deliberate, and it is also the trap. A company that certifies to ISO 42001 and writes a six-month retention period into its procedures has complied with the standard and failed the regulator, because the standard asks whether a period was defined and documented, while the regulator asks whether the records exist when they are needed. The procurement teams that buy AI systems are beginning to ask the same question the regulators ask, and the answer they want is not a citation to the standard. It is a number, and a reason for the number, and evidence that the number was followed.

The case for deleting early

The counterargument deserves a hearing, because it is the argument the privacy teams make, and it is not frivolous. Keeping records costs money, and AI decision logs are the most expensive records a company has ever kept, measured in volume. A model that makes ten thousand decisions a day generates a log that grows faster than any archive the company has built before, and the storage bill compounds. The privacy laws push the other way: the General Data Protection Regulation’s storage limitation principle requires personal data to be kept no longer than necessary, and a log that contains personal data cannot be kept forever simply because it might be useful. And there is a litigation argument for deletion: records that do not exist cannot be subpoenaed, and a company that keeps everything keeps the evidence of its own mistakes.

The counterargument fails on the arithmetic of the risk, not the principle. The storage cost of a decision log is measured in thousands of dollars a year. The cost of not having it, the legal bill in the opening story, the regulatory finding, the lost procurement deal, is measured in millions. The privacy argument is real but manageable: the logs can be pseudonymized, aggregated, and access-controlled, and the storage limitation principle asks whether the retention is necessary, not whether it is short. A retention period justified by a documented legal obligation is necessary by definition. And the litigation argument, that deletion protects the company, is the one argument that should frighten a general counsel most, because a retention policy designed to destroy evidence is the fastest route to an adverse inference in court.

A retention schedule that survives an audit

The practical version is a schedule, and it fits on one page. Keep the decision logs for at least the longest period any law that applies to you requires, and longer if your industry’s litigation horizon is longer than the law’s. Keep the technical documentation for ten years, matching the AI Act’s period, because the documentation is the record that explains the system when the logs are gone. Keep the model versions themselves, or at least the version identifiers and the training-data lineage, for as long as the documentation, because a log without the model that produced it is a receipt without a store. And write the schedule down, with the reason for each period, because a schedule that exists only in someone’s head is not a schedule.

The companies that will be asked this question are not the ones that did something wrong. They are the ones whose models made a decision that someone wants to understand, years after the fact, and the only thing standing between them and a satisfactory answer is whether the record still exists. The retention period is not a compliance cost. It is the price of being able to prove, three years from now, that the decision your model made was the right one. The bank in the opening story could not prove it, and the proof it could not give cost more than the storage ever would have.
