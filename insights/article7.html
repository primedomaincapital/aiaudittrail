NIST AI Risk Management Framework vs. ISO 42001: Which Audit Trail Satisfies Both?

NIST’s framework and ISO’s standard cover much of the same ground — and they are not the same thing. One is voluntary guidance; the other is a certificate buyers ask for by name. One audit trail can satisfy both, if it is built to.

by Prime Domain Capital Research · 2026-11-19

The vendor review meeting had been going well for forty minutes. The startup’s chief information security officer had walked the bank’s procurement team through the model registry, the bias testing, the incident log — a governance program built, line by line, on the National Institute of Standards and Technology’s AI Risk Management Framework. Then the bank’s third-party risk manager asked the question that ended the meeting’s good mood: “That’s helpful. But are you ISO 42001 certified?” The CISO said no. The risk manager wrote something down. Nobody in the room could say, in that moment, whether the two things were substitutes, complements, or entirely different jobs.

That confusion is now one of the most expensive misunderstandings in AI governance. NIST’s framework and ISO’s standard are the two documents every serious AI program cites, they share vocabulary, they cover much of the same ground — and they are not the same thing. One is voluntary guidance from a U.S. government laboratory. The other is a certifiable management system standard from an international body, audited by independent firms. Choose the wrong one for your buyer and you spend a year building evidence nobody asked for. Choose both, badly, and you run two programs that duplicate each other’s paperwork. The audit trail is where the two meet: it is the single record that can satisfy both, if it is built to.

Two documents, two jobs

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework, published as NIST.AI.100-1 on January 26, 2023, is voluntary guidance for managing risk across the life of an AI system. It organizes the work into four functions — Govern, Map, Measure, Manage — and breaks those into 19 categories and 72 subcategories. Govern runs across the whole organization: who owns AI risk, what the policies say, how much risk the company accepts. Map, Measure, and Manage apply system by system: what the system is for, who it touches, how its risks are tested, and what happens when a test fails. The framework also names seven characteristics of trustworthy AI that the four functions are meant to produce. NIST published a companion Generative AI Profile, NIST-AI-600-1, on July 26, 2024, adding twelve risk categories specific to generative systems, including confabulation, prompt injection, and data privacy. Nobody certifies conformance to any of it. There is no NIST AI RMF certificate, because the framework was written to be adopted, not audited.

ISO/IEC 42001:2023, published in December 2023, is the opposite kind of document. It specifies the requirements for an artificial intelligence management system — the policies, roles, risk processes, and improvement cycle an organization runs around its AI — and it is certifiable. An accredited certification body reviews the documentation in a Stage 1 audit, tests implementation in a Stage 2 audit, and issues a certificate valid for three years, with annual surveillance audits in between. The standard runs on clauses 4 through 10, the familiar management-system spine shared with ISO 27001, plus 38 controls in Annex A covering AI-specific risks. In July 2025, ISO/IEC 42006 set the rules for the bodies that audit it, closing the accreditation gap that had slowed the standard’s first two years. The certificate attests to the management system, not to any individual model: it says the organization governs its AI in a controlled way, not that a particular model is accurate or safe.

What each one asks you to write down

Strip away the preambles and both documents are, at their core, record-keeping demands — which is why one trail can serve both. NIST’s Govern asks for an inventory of AI systems, resourced according to risk, covering retired systems as well as live ones, plus written policies and named accountability. ISO 42001’s clauses 4 through 6 ask for the same inventory in different words: the scope of the management system, the organization’s context, leadership commitment, and a documented risk assessment. A registry that lists every system, its owner, its intended use, and its risk rating satisfies both at once.

Map and Measure are where the audit trail itself is born. NIST’s Map asks teams to document a system’s purpose, its stakeholders, and its limits before it ships. Measure asks for the test results — bias checks, security testing, performance monitoring — tracked over time, including after deployment. ISO 42001’s Annex A controls ask for the same evidence filed under control headings: impact assessments, data provenance, monitoring records, incident logs. The EU AI Act, which sits behind both documents for any company selling into Europe, asks for the same artifacts in statutory language: risk management under Article 9, technical documentation under Article 11, automatic logging under Article 12, a quality management system under Article 17, and post-market monitoring under Article 72. Three different vocabularies, one evidence pile.

Manage is the function most programs fake and most auditors test. NIST’s Manage 1.3 requires that responses to high-priority risks be developed, planned, and documented — mitigate, transfer, avoid, or accept, with the choice on the record. ISO 42001 requires the same treatment decisions, plus management review and continual improvement: the system must show that it learns from incidents. A policy document proves intent. The trail proves conduct.

One trail, two audits

The practical consequence is that a company should build one evidence store and map it twice, not build two programs. Start with the trail: every AI decision logged with its timestamp, model version, input and output, operator identity, and any risk flag triggered. Add the registry: every system, its owner, its purpose, its risk tier. Add the test record: what was measured before launch and what is measured still. Then write two mappings — one from that evidence to NIST’s 72 subcategories, one to ISO 42001’s 38 Annex A controls — and keep both mappings current. When the bank’s procurement team asks for the ISO certificate, the trail is the evidence the certification body audits. When a U.S. federal buyer asks how the company follows NIST, the same trail is the answer, organized under Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage.

The economics point the same way. NIST costs nothing to adopt: there is no fee, no audit, no certificate to buy, and the cost is entirely internal labour. ISO 42001 certification runs $46,000 to $80,000 or more in audit fees across the three-year cycle, on Schellman’s published figures — Stage 1 and Stage 2 in the $20,000s to the $40,000s, surveillance at $13,000 to $20,000 or more a year — with Vanta’s guide giving a lower $14,000 to $38,000 range, and four to twelve months from kickoff to certificate.

Where they part ways

The overlap is real, but the differences decide the purchase. The first is the certificate itself. The ISO Survey 2025, published in October 2026 with data as of December 31, 2025, recorded just 271 valid accredited ISO/IEC 42001 certificates worldwide, across 40 countries and 572 sites — the United States held 100 of them, 36.9% of the total — against 125,622 valid ISO 27001 certificates in the same survey. ISO 42001 is two years into issuance and still rare; that rarity is exactly why it carries weight in procurement. A buyer who writes “ISO 42001 certified” into a vendor questionnaire is not asking for a framework. They are asking for a certificate a third party signed, and no amount of NIST mapping satisfies that line.

The second difference is legal force. Neither document is law, and neither gives a company a presumption of conformity with the EU AI Act. ISO 42001 is not a harmonised standard under the Act, so certification alone does not create that presumption; the first harmonised standard for the Act, EN 18286:2026, was only cited in October 2026. NIST’s framework has no European legal standing at all. A company that buys ISO 42001 believing it has bought EU AI Act compliance has bought something valuable and misunderstood what it bought.

The third difference is momentum, and it cuts both ways. ISO 42001 adoption is accelerating from a tiny base: fewer than a hundred organizations held a certificate in January 2026 and roughly 350 did by spring, and the names now include Amazon Web Services, certified in November 2024 with Schellman auditing under ANAB accreditation, Anthropic in January 2025, Intercom in April 2025, Salesforce in October 2025 covering eighteen products including Agentforce and Slack AI, plus ServiceNow and Snowflake. NIST, meanwhile, is being revised: the July 2025 America’s AI Action Plan directed NIST to revise AI RMF 1.0, and as of September 2026 no draft had been published.

A policy document proves intent. The trail proves conduct — and conduct is what both audits test.

The case for NIST alone

The counterargument to certifying at all is strong, and many serious teams make it. If your buyers are American, if your systems are internal, if no procurement questionnaire has ever mentioned ISO 42001, then a NIST-aligned program with a rigorous audit trail gives you nearly all of the risk management at a fraction of the cost — and the certificate would be a badge in search of an audience. NIST’s framework is also the more flexible instrument: it was written to be adapted to any sector and any system, while ISO 42001 demands a management system with all the process overhead that phrase implies. For a company whose AI estate is five internal tools and a vendor chatbot, the management system can cost more than the risk it manages.

That argument fails in exactly one situation, and it is the situation that matters most: when the buyer on the other side of the table has already decided. Enterprise procurement, regulated-industry vendor review, and European buyers increasingly treat ISO 42001 as a threshold requirement, the way ISO 27001 became one a decade ago. The AICPA and the Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company announced a joint certification on October 19, 2026 combining SOC 2 with an AI assessment that maps to ISO 42001, the NIST framework, and the EU AI Act — a sign that the market is converging on a world where the certificate is the ticket and the framework is the method.

Build the trail once, map it twice

The decision, then, is not which document is better. It is which buyer you answer to first. If your next twelve months are dominated by enterprise procurement and European customers, certify to ISO 42001 and use the NIST framework as the internal method for running the program — the certificate opens the door, the framework keeps the program honest. If your buyers are American, your systems are internal, and nobody has asked for a certificate, build the NIST-aligned trail now and keep the ISO mapping warm, so certification is a four-to-twelve-month project rather than a rebuild when the first buyer asks.

Either way, the work is the same work, and the order is the same order. Inventory the systems. Log the decisions. Test before launch and monitor after. Record the treatment of every risk that matters. That is the audit trail, and it is the only artifact both frameworks, both kinds of auditor, and eventually the regulator will ask to see. The frameworks will keep changing — NIST is being revised, ISO’s harmonised status is unresolved, the EU’s deadlines have already moved once. A trail built to survive a change of framework outlasts every one of them.
